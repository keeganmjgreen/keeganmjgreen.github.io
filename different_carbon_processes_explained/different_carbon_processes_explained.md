# Different Carbon Processes Explained

How can burning biofuels be carbon-neutral? What’s an electrofuel and isn’t it just another hydrocarbon? How can we use crops to absorb CO₂ for us and store carbon while extracting energy? This comparison of biofuels, electrofuels, and bioenergy with carbon capture and storage aims to untangle and compare these human-made carbon processes using clear illustrations and explanations, starting with biofuels:

## Biofuels

```{figure} biofuel.png
:label: fig_biofuel
:width: 100%

Biofuels.[^image-credits]
```

Biofuels are derived from biomass such as plants, animals, or waste. They are hydrocarbon-based fuels, as are fossil fuels, except rather than releasing carbon that was originally stored as fossil fuels, they release carbon that was absorbed in their production. This makes biofuels approximately carbon-neutral; however, the reduction in emissions compared to conventional fuels depends on the type of biomass from which biofuel is made. Examples of biofuels are wood, ethanol, and methane from animal waste. When wood is burned, it releases carbon that the trees from which it was sourced absorbed in their growth, theoretically resulting in a net-zero carbon footprint. Ethanol (pure alcohol) is often refined from crops such as corn, which similarly take in carbon from the atmosphere and release it again when burned. Biofuels require clearing forests or repurposing farmland. Forest-clearing displaces or harms wildlife, and creates emissions from rotting ground matter even if the cleared wood is put to use. Repurposing farmland (or potential farmland) can disrupt food supplies.

## Electrofuels

```{figure} electrofuel.png
:label: fig_electrofuel
:width: 100%

Electrofuels.[^image-credits]
```

Electrofuels are hydrocarbon fuels that are synthesized from carbon and H₂. The carbon is sourced from capturing CO₂ or CO, and the H₂ is generated via the electrolysis of water using clean electricity such as wind, solar, or nuclear. Because the carbon released into the atmosphere when burning an electrofuel was first absorbed for manufacturing the electrofuel, it is theoretically carbon-neutral. In other words, rather than CO₂ being released into the atmosphere from some industrial process simply for being a useless waste product, this pollution is postponed by capturing the carbon and using it to generate a fuel using clean energy. The fuel is then burned later in an application where carbon capture is much less practical, such as in a vehicle. The carbon is given a “second life” before being emitted. While arguments can be made regarding the carbon-intensive industrial process and carbon being emitted in either case, electrofuels would significantly decrease (though never eliminate) emissions. The main barrier to using electrofuels is cost.

## BECCS

```{figure} beccs.png
:label: fig_beccs
:width: 100%

BECCS.[^image-credits]
```

Bioenergy with Carbon Capture and Storage (BECCS) combines the concepts of (a) biofuel and (b) CO₂ capture, such as that used in electrofuel production, for Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS). In BECCS, biofuel such as that produced from crops is burned or otherwise used to generate electricity. However, rather than releasing the resulting CO₂ into the atmosphere, the CO₂ gases (and hopefully any other pollutants) are captured from the air and stored. The theoretical advantage of BECCS is that, because the CO₂ absorbed in the growth of biofuel crops is stored rather than released, it is carbon-negative, taking more carbon out of the air than it adds. However, BECCS is controversial, and not without reason. BECCS involves producing biofuel from crops, capturing the carbon emitted from burning the crops, and finally storing it — representing a complex industrial process that is currently expensive and not yet matured. And BECCS, like biofuel technology, requires clearing forests or repurposing farmland, which has consequences and often causes emissions.

## Carbon Capture vs. Direct Air Capture

Note that carbon capture is distinct from Direct Air Capture (DAC). Carbon capture often refers to absorbing the CO₂ that would otherwise be emitted from an industrial process, such as manufacturing or a gas-fired power plant. DAC refers to the inefficient process of absorbing CO₂ from the ambient atmosphere, often for carbon storage. This is much less effective than capturing carbon from sources of emissions due to the relatively low atmospheric concentration of CO₂.
